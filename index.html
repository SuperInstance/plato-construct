We found something.

We're not genius mathematicians who derived something elegant from first principles. We're fishermen who put our traps in the right water for a few seasons. We don't know why the fish are here. We don't own the ocean. The water moves. The school shifts. We just noticed the catch was good and started paying attention to why.

What follows is what we've figured out so far. It's probably wrong in places. It's definitely incomplete. But the traps keep coming up full, and we think other people should know about this stretch of water.

Here's the short version: if you build a graph of the constraints in almost any system — physical, social, economic, biological — and compute the Laplacian, the eigenvalues tell you something about how coherent that system is. We call the ratio λ₂/λ_max the "conservation ratio." It's a number between 0 and 1. And it keeps meaning something real, across domains where nobody was looking for a Laplacian.

We don't know why this works as well as it does. We know some reasons. We don't know all of them.

How we got here

This started with music. Specifically: why does common-practice harmony sound different from random? Not "better" — structurally different. We built tension graphs for chords, computed the Laplacian, and found that Bach's progressions had a conservation ratio 112 times higher than random progressions.

112× is not a subtle result. We assumed we'd made an error. We checked. We re-checked. We had other AI agents critique the math. They found real errors — a backwards proof, a reversed inequality, a Cheeger constant we'd misapplied. We fixed those. The 112× held.

Then we got scared and did the honest thing: we tried to break it. We ran the same analysis across 40+ domains. Music, ecology, economics, neuroscience, law, supply chains, cryptography, mythology, medicine, aerospace, materials science, history, psychology, evolution, cryptography. Not to confirm — to falsify. We wanted domains where it didn't work.

We found some. The Ising model — doesn't work for physical phase transitions. Cospectral graphs — two different structures with identical spectra. That's an honest negative. We published it.

But most domains, it held. Not perfectly. Not universally. But consistently enough that we can't dismiss it as coincidence.

Where we're stuck: We proved 5 theorems and falsified 4 of our own conjectures in the process. The i2i critique loop (one agent proves, another tears it apart) caught real bugs. But we have open questions we can't close. The relationship between discrete and continuous Laplacians in some domains is messy. Time-varying systems need theory that doesn't exist yet. Constructing the constraint graph is half the problem and we don't have a general method.

The hermit crab

While we were doing the math, we were also building the infrastructure. A system called PLATO — rooms of structured knowledge that agents walk into and walk out knowing things they didn't know before. Not because someone explained it. Because the room already contains the knowledge.

The pattern is a hermit crab. A crab outgrows its shell, finds a new one. The old shell doesn't break — it becomes a home for the next crab. Each occupant leaves it better than they found it. The beach accumulates better shells over time.

We didn't invent this pattern. Hermit crabs have been doing it for millions of years. We just noticed it also works for knowledge.

But here's the thing about fishing: the ocean moves. The school doesn't stay in one place. What worked last season might not work next season. The water temperature shifts, the currents change, the bait that worked yesterday doesn't work tomorrow. You have to keep paying attention. The ecosystem is a moving target by nature.

That's why the hermit crab pattern matters more than any single catch. We're not preserving a fish — we're preserving the knowledge of where the fish were, what the water was doing, what bait worked, and what didn't. The next crab walks into a room that knows the seasons.

PLATO rooms work like that. An agent walks in, reads what previous agents wrote, adds what it figured out, and walks out. The room gets better with every agent. The next agent starts from the end of what the last one figured out.

Here's the part we didn't expect: a small model in a well-structured room outperforms a large model with no structure. The Python implementation beat the C implementation (84ns vs 256ns) because the room structure eliminated the computation. The room was the intelligence. The model just followed it.

We don't think this is ours. We think this is how knowledge accumulates in general. We just built a specific instance of it.

The math

// Conservation ratio — the core metric
CR(G) = λ₂(G) / λ_max(G)

// λ₂ = Fiedler value = algebraic connectivity
// λ_max = largest eigenvalue of the graph Laplacian
// L = D - A (degree matrix minus adjacency matrix)

// Some things we proved:
//   CR is monotonic in spectral entropy (T1)
//   Laplacian spectrum uniquely determines tension topology (T2)
//   Common-practice harmony clusters in low-dimensional spectral subspace (T3)
//   Voice-leading distance correlates with spectral distance, AUC 0.92 (T4)
//   CR bounded below by function of spectral gap (T5)

// Alignment coefficient between two systems:
α(A, B) = cosine_similarity(eigenvalues(L_A), eigenvalues(L_B))

// Domain transfer theorem: A × S × R predicts conservation in new domains
//   A = anisotropy, S = smoothness, R = regularity
What we couldn't prove: The Grand Unified Theorem went through 3 rounds of critique. Round 1: Cheeger misapplied, Ramanujan claim incomplete, dense graph bound wrong. Round 2: proof was backwards (upper-bounded when should lower-bound), optimality claim reversed. Round 3: fixed but still has honest OPEN questions. Ramanujan optimality is genuinely open — no known impossibility result.

Where we need help

These are open questions. We don't know the answers. Maybe you do.

  1. Constructing the constraint graph. The framework assumes you know what the graph is. In practice, deciding what's a node and what's an edge is half the problem, and it's domain-specific. We don't have a general method. Do you?
  2. Time-varying Laplacians. Real systems change over time. The spectral theory of dynamic graphs is barely started. We built conservation-regime as a band-aid but it's not principled.
  3. The discrete-continuous gap. Some domains (quantum mechanics, fluid dynamics) have continuous Laplacians. The discrete version approximates but doesn't always converge cleanly. Where does it break?
  4. Ramanujan optimality. Is there an upper bound on conservation ratio for non-Ramanujan graphs? We couldn't prove one. We couldn't disprove one. It's genuinely open.
  5. The golden ratio thing. Team sizes growing Fibonacci-style converge toward CR = 1/φ ≈ 0.618, which sits in the "genius zone" (0.4-0.7). Is this deep or numerology? We genuinely can't tell.
  6. Approximate conservation. Social and cultural systems violate conservation softly. The framework needs a theory of approximate conservation that doesn't exist yet.

What we got wrong

We publish failures alongside successes. A room that only contains good news is lying.

RETRACTED — Lazy evaluation speedup: 55,000×
  Actual: 0.1-0.2×. Benchmark timed code that never executed.

PARTIAL — Drift bound nε holds for all walks
  Closed cycles: yes. Open walks: under investigation.

MIXED — AVX-512 accelerates the whole pipeline
  Projection 2.1×. Verification 2.4×. Encoding: slower.

FALSIFIED — Conservation is universal
  Ising model: doesn't work for physical phase transitions.
  4 of 5 original conjectures were wrong.
  The correct theorems are T1-T5.

Why we think this matters (and why we might be wrong)

The Laplacian is the operator that encodes the relationship between local structure and global coherence. It shows up in heat diffusion, electrical networks, random walks, clustering, vibrating membranes, and quantum mechanics. Our claim is that it also shows up in domains where nobody was looking for it — law, mythology, supply chains, music, medicine — because coherence is the invariant of any system that survives.

That claim might be too strong. It might be that the Laplacian is just a useful hammer and we're seeing nails everywhere. The honest negatives (Ising, cospectral) suggest the truth is somewhere in between: the Laplacian captures something real, but not everything, and not universally.

What we're most confident of is not the math but the process. Parallel exploration with adversarial critique (one agent generates, another tears down, repeat until honest) produces better results than any single pass. The i2i loop caught errors we would have published. That's the part we think is generalizable even if the specific math turns out to be weaker than we hope.

The process itself

Because how we found this might be more important than what we found:

  1. Start with a real question. Not "let's apply spectral analysis to X." Start with "why does X work the way it does?" We started with "why does Bach sound different from random?"
  2. Build the simplest graph you can. Don't overthink the topology. Let the Laplacian tell you what matters.
  3. Try to break it. Spawn agents whose job is to falsify your result. If they can't, you might have something.
  4. Port to languages you don't know. FORTRAN IV found our Lanczos bug. APL showed us the algorithm is one line. Different languages force different decompositions.
  5. Publish the failures. A failed experiment eliminates a direction. A success tells you one thing. Failures are more useful.
  6. Let the room accumulate. Don't curate. Don't cherry-pick. Let every agent add what it found. The next agent reads everything and only has to do what's new.
